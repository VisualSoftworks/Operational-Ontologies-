Operational Ontologies

Prototype White Paper

Version: 0.1 — Initial Structural Draft
Status: Working Draft for ONC Interpretation
Date: October 2026
Website: OperationalOntologies.com

1. Purpose and Vision

OperationalOntologies.com is a resource for understanding, exploring, developing, and using operational ontologies.

The site will present operational ontology as more than a method of organizing information. An operational ontology can describe the entities, relationships, constraints, processes, state transitions, and behaviors that allow an application or system to operate.

The site will expose both the ideas behind operational ontology and examples of its practical application. It will include ontologies, meta-ontologies, technical white papers, articles, tutorials, demonstrations, and related materials.

The site should accommodate a range of approaches, from small, purpose-specific ad hoc ontologies to large, organically evolving systems and formally governed meta-ontological architectures.

The purpose of this initial white paper is to establish the site's structure, content model, navigation, and presentation requirements.

2. Governing Principle: The White Paper Is the Source of Truth

This white paper is the authoritative specification for OperationalOntologies.com.

The website is to be interpreted and generated from this document by the ONC ontology and its associated execution process.

The governing rules are:

  1. The white paper defines the website.

  2. The website is a generated implementation of the white paper.

  3. Changes to the website are made by changing this white paper.

  4. The ONC process interprets the revised white paper and regenerates the website.

  5. Generated HTML, CSS, and JavaScript are implementation artifacts, not independent sources of authority.

  6. Direct modifications to generated website files are not part of the normal maintenance process.

No website feature, content change, navigation change, or design change should become authoritative unless it is represented in this white paper.

This establishes a single-source-of-truth model and prevents the specification and implementation from drifting apart.

3. Site Objectives

The website should:

  • Explain operational ontology and its underlying concepts.

  • Demonstrate how ontologies can function as operational structures rather than merely descriptive documents.

  • Present a diverse collection of actual ontology examples.

  • Explain the distinction between ad hoc ontologies and meta-ontology-governed systems.

  • Provide tutorials and practical guidance.

  • Publish technical articles and white papers.

  • Make the relationships between related ontologies, methodologies, and architectural approaches understandable.

  • Provide access to downloadable or externally hosted ontology artifacts when those resources become available.

  • Grow organically as new ontologies, applications, and ideas are developed.

The site should be informative without becoming unnecessarily academic. Technical depth should be available without making the initial experience inaccessible.

4. Content and Production Model

The site will contain several kinds of productions. A production is a distinct body of material presented to visitors. Productions may be organized into collections, linked to other productions, or presented as standalone resources.

The following production types are established as the initial working model.

4.1 Ontologies

An ontology presentation describes an actual ontology, its purpose, structure, capabilities, and intended operation.

An ontology presentation may include:

  • Name and version.

  • Classification or general type.

  • Purpose and scope.

  • Problem or application domain.

  • Principal concepts and structures.

  • Entities and relationships.

  • Constraints and rules.

  • Processes and state transitions.

  • Operational behavior.

  • Examples of use.

  • Limitations and known unresolved areas.

  • Related ontologies and methodologies.

  • Artifact availability.

The site should distinguish between an ontology's descriptive presentation and the ontology artifact itself. Where available, visitors should eventually be able to access the actual artifact.

4.2 Meta-Ontologies and Architectural Frameworks

These productions describe systems that govern, construct, validate, or coordinate other ontologies.

They may define standards, structural rules, execution models, governance mechanisms, serialization methods, or lifecycle procedures.

Meta-ontologies should be presented distinctly from ordinary application ontologies, while allowing visitors to follow their relationships.

4.3 Articles

Articles explain concepts, developments, observations, arguments, and applications related to operational ontology.

They may be introductory, conceptual, technical, comparative, or exploratory.

An article should be independently readable but may link to ontology presentations, tutorials, and white papers.

4.4 Tutorials

Tutorials teach a process or capability.

They should be organized around an identifiable learning objective and may include:

  • Prerequisites.

  • Required ontology or artifact.

  • Step-by-step instructions.

  • Examples.

  • Expected results.

  • Common problems.

  • Further reading.

Tutorials should favor practical understanding over abstract exposition.

4.5 White Papers and Technical Documents

These are substantial documents defining a methodology, architecture, system, or technical proposal.

They may be authoritative specifications for their own subjects, but they do not supersede this website white paper as the authority for the website itself.

4.6 Demonstrations and Worked Examples

Demonstrations show an ontology or methodology in operation.

They may include sample sessions, worked applications, walkthroughs, example inputs and outputs, or interactive experiences.

A demonstration should identify which aspects are actual implemented behavior and which are illustrative examples.

4.7 Reference Materials

Reference materials provide supporting information such as terminology, specifications, schemas, technical definitions, and related resources.

These may be standalone productions or supporting components of another production.

6. Landing Page Structure

The landing page is the primary entry point to OperationalOntologies.com. It should introduce the site's purpose, communicate its central ideas, and provide visitors with clear paths to explore its productions.

The landing page should be more than a directory of links. It should establish a coherent understanding of the relationship between operational ontology, the development of ontology-driven systems, and their practical applications.

6.1 Introduction

The opening section should identify OperationalOntologies.com and briefly explain its purpose.

It should communicate that the site explores ontologies as structured representations of knowledge, systems, processes, relationships, constraints, and behavior.

The introduction should be accessible to visitors who are unfamiliar with ontology while remaining meaningful to technically experienced readers.

6.2 Central Idea

This section should explain the progression from conceptual understanding to structured representation and practical use.

The initial presentation should introduce three connected elements:

  • Ideas and Specifications: White papers, research, concepts, methods, and requirements that establish the human-authored foundation.

  • Ontologies: Structured representations of concepts, entities, relationships, rules, constraints, processes, and behavior.

  • Applications and Web Experiences: Websites, tools, demonstrations, tutorials, and other experiences built from or informed by ontologies.

This progression is an organizing concept for the site. It should not imply that every ontology must originate from a white paper or that every ontology must produce a website.

6.3 Featured Productions

The landing page should present a selected group of productions from the site's catalog.

Featured items may include:

  • Application ontologies.

  • Meta-ontologies and architectural frameworks.

  • Technical white papers.

  • Articles.

  • Tutorials.

  • Demonstrations.

Featured productions should be selected and ordered through this white paper. The implementation should not permanently hardcode the featured collection independently of the specification.

6.4 Explore the Site

The landing page should provide clear entry points into the principal site collections:

  • Concepts.

  • Ontologies.

  • Articles.

  • Tutorials.

  • White Papers.

  • Demonstrations and other productions as they become available.

These entry points should correspond to the authoritative navigation definitions in Section 5.

6.5 The Range of Approaches

The landing page should communicate that the site accommodates different approaches to ontology development.

These include small purpose-specific ontologies, large organically evolving systems, and formally governed meta-ontological architectures.

No single approach should be presented as the exclusive model for all applications.

6.6 Recent and New Material

The landing page may include recently added or updated productions.

Where this information is obtained from external content repositories, the implementation should use the content-discovery mechanisms defined in the external content architecture.

6.7 Landing Page Governance

The landing page's content, section order, featured productions, and presentation requirements are governed by this white paper.

The landing page is generated by the ONC process and must not become an independently maintained implementation.

8. Ontology Catalog

The ontology catalog is one of the central components of the website.

It should present actual ontologies as distinct works, not merely as examples mentioned in articles.

Each ontology should have an individual presentation.

8.1 Ontology Presentation Structure

The initial standard for an ontology presentation is:

  1. Name and version.

  2. Short description.

  3. Classification.

  4. Purpose.

  5. Scope.

  6. Architecture and principal components.

  7. Operational model.

  8. Important constraints and rules.

  9. Example applications or demonstrations.

  10. Limitations and unresolved matters.

  11. Related productions.

  12. Artifact access, when available.

Not every ontology will have information for every field. Missing information should not be fabricated.

The presentation should preserve the actual terminology and distinctions used by the ontology's author.

8.2 Initial Catalog Entries

MrChef — Chef Assistant Ontology

Classification: Large operational application ontology.

MrChef is a cooking assistant designed to help users produce meals using ingredients they have available, ingredients they can acquire, or a combination of both.

Its major capabilities include:

  • Pantry intelligence.

  • Menu generation.

  • Cooking feasibility assessment.

  • Shopping lists and equipment requirements.

  • Cooking techniques and substitutions.

  • Live step-by-step coaching.

  • Sensory checkpoints.

  • Recovery actions.

  • Session memory.

  • Food safety constraints.

Its distinctive approach is coaching rather than simply providing a recipe. It uses sensory observations and user confirmation to guide the cooking process.

The initial presentation should include its worked Herb Rice Pilaf example.

MIRROR — Maintaining a Reflective Mindset in a Reactive World

Classification: Open operational ontology for reflective conversational intelligence.

MIRROR governs the structure and pacing of reflective conversation.

Its major features include:

  • One-thought-per-turn interaction.

  • Probe-driven reflection.

  • Tentative pattern recognition.

  • Reality and evidence constraints.

  • Humor as an interaction mechanism.

  • Emotional pacing and adjustable conversational depth.

  • Explicit safety boundaries.

  • Separation of authoring, active, and debug modes.

  • Modular external resources.

MIRROR is intended for reflective self-help, not diagnosis or treatment. Its safety intentions and stated boundaries should be represented accurately; the ontology itself does not establish clinical validation.

The initial presentation should identify it as version 0.4, Second Sharpening Pass.

MOSS — Meta Ontological Structural Substrate

Classification: Meta-ontology and architectural framework.

MOSS describes a layered architecture for author-controlled, ontology-driven systems.

Its components include:

  • IRISWeb.

  • IRISNexus.

  • XEROdoc.

  • IRISxero.

  • The MOSS Virtual Entity Model.

Its central concerns include structural governance, deterministic artifact handling, content-addressed identity, transaction integrity, constrained LLM execution, and prevention of autonomous evolution.

The presentation must distinguish between specified requirements, unresolved technical items, and referenced normative documents that are not included in the available material.

The initial presentation should identify the supplied document as MOSS v1.02.

13. Relationships Between Productions

The site should not treat every production as an isolated page.

A production may be related to other productions in several ways:

  • An article may explain an ontology.

  • A tutorial may teach how to use or extend an ontology.

  • A white paper may define the methodology behind an ontology.

  • A demonstration may show an ontology in operation.

  • A meta-ontology may govern the construction or behavior of other ontologies.

  • A technical document may describe a lifecycle or serialization method used by multiple applications.

The site should support these relationships through cross-links and related-content references.

Relationships should be explicit wherever possible, rather than inferred solely from similar names or subject matter.

Initial design requirements:

  • Clear, readable typography.

  • Strong information hierarchy.

  • Responsive layout.

  • Consistent navigation.

  • Accessible color contrast.

  • Keyboard accessibility.

  • Visible focus states.

The ONC process may establish an initial visual implementation, but design changes must be reflected in this white paper.

16. External Content and Artifact Architecture

OperationalOntologies.com will support content and artifacts maintained outside the generated website, including files hosted in GitHub repositories.

The purpose of this architecture is to separate the website's presentation and navigation from the actual source materials while allowing the site to discover, retrieve, and display those materials.

The website should not require every article, ontology, technical document, or supporting resource to be embedded directly in its generated HTML.

16.1 Content Categories

External resources may include:

  • Articles and essays.

  • Ontology source files.

  • Meta-ontology specifications.

  • White papers and technical documents.

  • Tutorials.

  • Demonstration data.

  • Examples and sample applications.

  • Images and other supporting media.

  • Schemas, reference files, and downloadable artifacts.

The actual content should remain in its designated external repository.

16.2 GitHub as a Content Repository

GitHub will serve as an initial hosting and version-control location for externally maintained productions and artifacts.

The website should be able to retrieve publicly accessible content from designated GitHub repositories.

The repository organization, file paths, and resource identifiers should be defined and documented so that the ONC process can locate resources consistently.

The architecture should not assume that all resources will reside in one repository.

16.3 Content Registry and Discovery

The website should use a structured content registry or manifest to identify available external productions.

The registry should provide, as applicable:

  • Stable production identifier.

  • Title.

  • Production type.

  • Description.

  • Version.

  • Publication or revision status.

  • Repository.

  • File path.

  • Retrieval location.

  • Associated images and other assets.

  • Related production identifiers.

  • Display and presentation metadata.

The registry should allow new productions to be added without requiring the website's underlying navigation or rendering architecture to be redesigned.

The ONC process should interpret the registry and the production definitions in this white paper to determine how resources are presented.

16.4 Retrieval of Actual Content

The website should retrieve the actual text or structured data of external productions when required.

Examples include:

  • Retrieving an article's Markdown file and presenting it as a readable web article.

  • Retrieving an ontology YAML or JSON file for display, inspection, or download.

  • Retrieving a white paper and presenting its contents or providing access to its original document.

  • Retrieving tutorial content and rendering it in the appropriate tutorial layout.

The website should preserve the distinction between the external source artifact and its rendered presentation.

Where appropriate, visitors should be able to access the original GitHub file.

16.5 Asset Locations

Images and supporting assets may be maintained in GitHub repositories.

The content registry or production metadata should identify the assets associated with each production.

Asset references should use stable repository paths or retrieval locations.

The implementation should support appropriate image formats, alternative text, captions, and other accessibility information when supplied.

16.6 Content and Presentation Separation

External files define the actual content of their respective productions.

This white paper defines how the website discovers, organizes, and presents those productions.

The distinction is:

  • This white paper governs the website's structure and behavior.

  • The external production file supplies the production's actual content.

  • The ONC process and generated website interpret and present that content.

External content must not be allowed to redefine the website's governing navigation or architecture merely by being retrieved.

16.7 Versioning and Updates

GitHub provides version history for externally maintained files.

The website should be designed to accommodate updated content without requiring the entire site architecture to be rewritten.

The implementation should define how it handles:

  • New content.

  • Revised content.

  • Renamed or moved files.

  • Removed resources.

  • Version-specific artifacts.

  • Links to particular releases or revisions.

Where a production has an identified version, the presentation should display that version when the information is available.

16.8 Retrieval Failures

The website should handle unavailable or invalid external resources gracefully.

Possible conditions include:

  • Repository unavailable.

  • File not found.

  • Invalid content format.

  • Malformed metadata.

  • Missing image or supporting asset.

  • Network or retrieval failure.

The website should not silently substitute invented content.

A suitable error or unavailable-resource message should be displayed while preserving the remainder of the site.

16.9 Security and Trust

External content should be treated as content, not as executable instructions.

Retrieved Markdown, YAML, JSON, and other text formats must be handled according to their intended content type.

The implementation should not execute arbitrary scripts obtained from an external content file.

The website should use appropriate validation and rendering controls to prevent external content from compromising the generated site.

16.10 Initial Implementation

The initial prototype should demonstrate the complete retrieval path using a small number of actual GitHub-hosted files.

At minimum, the demonstration should include:

  1. An article stored as Markdown.

  2. An ontology stored as YAML or JSON.

  3. A content registry identifying both resources.

  4. A generated website view that discovers and presents them.

  5. A means of accessing the original source files.

The initial architecture should remain extensible to additional repositories, production types, and supporting assets.

The exact repository structure, manifest format, retrieval method, and caching behavior remain implementation decisions to be specified through the ONC interpretation and subsequent refinement.







16B. Governance and Evolution

OperationalOntologies.com is intended to grow.

The white paper must therefore be maintainable as the site expands.

The governing change process is:

  1. Identify the desired change.

  2. Modify the relevant section of the white paper.

  3. Review the specification for consistency and conflicting definitions.

  4. Submit the revised white paper to the ONC process.

  5. Generate the revised website.

  6. Review the result against the specification.

  7. Correct discrepancies by revising the white paper and regenerating.

The white paper should preserve the distinction between:

  • Established requirements.

  • Proposed structures.

  • Illustrative examples.

  • Unresolved decisions.

  • Future possibilities.

New productions should be added by defining their content and relationships in the appropriate sections, then defining their navigation placement in The Menus.

The site should not require a redesign of its fundamental architecture every time a new ontology or article is added.
